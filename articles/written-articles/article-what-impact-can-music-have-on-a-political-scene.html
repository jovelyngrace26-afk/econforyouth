What Impact Can Music Have on a Political Scene?

From freedom songs to punk to The Troubles: how music has helped political movements spread, go mainstream and stay in everyday life.

SO
Sophia O’Kelly Published November 17, 2026 · 4 min read

Music is something that connects people globally and has done so for nearly the whole history of humanity. Music is something that can make us feel a wide range of emotions, ranging from love and euphoria to anger and hatred. Whilst music is rarely a catalyst or leader in political scenes and movements, it has often garnered support for movements and has helped to make political movements mainstream and a forceful, constant presence in everyday life. It ranges from influencing things such as the Civil Rights Movement, the punk scene (which is very political by nature), and The Troubles in Ireland. I’ll be discussing the impact that music has had on a few movements.[1]

The Civil Rights Movement

One of the most prominent examples is undoubtedly the Civil Rights Movement in America during the 60s. The concept of the teenager was invented in the 50s, and with that came a teen culture and the introduction of music to the mainstream public through records, TV, and radio. First, it was for enjoyment; then, with the cultural boom in the 60s, it became used to demand change. During the era of slavery in America, music was used to communicate ideas about freedom to other slaves, especially as a form of resistance through the Underground Railroad, and the Civil Rights Movement employed music again as a form of resistance and national education. Martin Luther King Jr. said in his 1964 book Why We Can’t Wait that civil rights activists:

“sing the freedom songs today for the same reason the slaves sang them, because we too are in bondage and the songs add hope to our determination that ‘We shall overcome, Black and white together, we shall overcome someday.’”[2]

The unofficial song of the Civil Rights Movement, “We Shall Overcome”, despite its roots being about labour struggles in the 1940s, was described by Wyatt Tee Walker as a unifying song, sung by “mass meetings”, “old women on their way to work”, “students...as they were being dragged away to jail”, highlighting the universal nature of music when employed by everyone for a singular cause. Whilst everyone knows about MLK Jr’s “I Have a Dream” speech, said during the 1963 March on Washington, there was a folk, jazz and blues singer on stage with him, Odetta, who sang “Oh Freedom”, a 1906 song that was created in response to race riots, illustrating how powerful words didn’t just come in the form of speech, but also music.

Punk and the Working Class

Another example of music directly influencing an entire subculture is punk, which reflected the political desire for greater socialism from the working class, fuelled by Margaret Thatcher. The rise of punk collates at the same time of the rise of the Sex Pistols, drawing inspiration from American glam rock and similar themes to the Civil Rights Movement itself. It became popularised partly because of the mainstream success of Anarchy in the UK and God Save the Queen (which the BBC blocked from becoming number 1 on the charts, and which many retailers refused to sell), and partly because of the aestheticisation of the working class and punk by Sex Pistols manager McLaren and famed fashion designer and girlfriend at the time Vivienne Westwood.[3] Whilst The Clash brought genuine notice to Britain’s urban struggles, and the punk movement split into sub-genres such as goth, riot grrrl, and more melancholic music such as Joy Division, the punk scene brought national notice to the working class: for good or for bad.

Music and The Troubles

During a similar period of time, music was arising in the UK and the Republic of Ireland about The Troubles, the intense, deadly, and formative period of struggle during the 21st century. One of the most popular Nationalist songs was “The Rising of the Moon”, covered by the Clancy Brothers and the Dubliners, which made it popular during The Troubles. The song actually dates back to 1798, when United Irishmen fought against the British, hence why it became symbolic. Naturally, there were songs about Bloody Sunday (1972), a defining event of The Troubles where British troops shot and killed/injured over a dozen peaceful protestors. Both John Lennon and Paul McCartney of The Beatles, the most influential band of all time, wrote songs about the event, both about anti-British involvement in Northern Ireland. Lennon sides with the IRA and is much more graphic about the event than U2’s song of the same name, whilst McCartney’s song “Give Ireland back to the Irish”, spurred by family connections in Ireland, was banned in Britain. This showcases how even music from those not directly linked to the movement can be just as impactful, especially coming from some of the most famous musicians of all time. Moreover, U2, and especially Bono, with his Catholic father and Protestant Mother, aimed for peace and collaboration between the two sides, and were very anti-conflict.[4]

Conclusion

Whilst music’s use wasn’t limited to 21st-century examples, and whilst it doesn’t only include the few examples I talked about, some of the examples I talked about brought along some very famous songs and cultural movements, and therefore were significant. To conclude, music was used during times of movements, either political, militaristic, or social, to either inspire change, become the leading voice of people, or for a shift towards peace, harmony, and understanding.
