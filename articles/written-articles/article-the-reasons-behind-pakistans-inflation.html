The Reasons Behind Pakistan's Inflation

What's driving rising prices in Pakistan, and how is inflation affecting everyday life?

AM
Asadullah bin Munir Published November 5, 2026 · 5 min read

Introduction

While inflation can often just be seen as increasing prices in general, in Pakistan, it is putting pressure on many families who are struggling to get by. The average wage for employees in 2024–25 was around PKR 39,042 per month (Pakistan Bureau of Statistics (PBS)).[1] This has a large impact on how comfortably people can afford their essential needs.

According to the World Bank, Pakistan had an annual consumer price inflation rate of 19.87% in 2022. This increased to 30.77% in 2023 before decreasing to 12.63% in 2024 and 3.55% in 2025.[2] While the inflation rate has decreased, it does not mean that prices have returned to their previous levels. It simply means that prices are not increasing as quickly. It is important to keep essential goods affordable while also improving people’s incomes.

Main Reasons for Inflation in Pakistan

1. Fiscal Deficits and Monetary Pressures

Pakistan has suffered from high inflation partly due to having a large fiscal deficit. The fiscal deficit in FY2024 was 6.9% of GDP, compared to 7.8% in FY2023 (Pakistan Economic Survey 2024–25). The tax-to-GDP ratio was around 9.6% in FY2024.[3]

This means the government has to borrow money or find other ways to fund its spending. Depending on how this is financed, it can increase inflation by adding more money into circulation without a corresponding increase in the production of goods and services.

The Economic Survey highlighted that the growth in broad money supply (M2) was 4.5% between July and March in FY2025, compared to 7.2% during the same period last year. The government needs to ensure sustainable public finances, improve tax collection, and invest in more productive areas.

2. Energy Prices

Pakistan’s energy sector has also contributed to inflation. In September 2025, an announcement was made about plans to resolve PKR 1.225 trillion in power sector circular debt.[4]

Between July and April 2024–25, housing, water, electricity, gas, and other fuels made up 23.6% of the national CPI basket (Economic Survey). This means that changes in utility and petroleum prices can increase people’s bills, as well as production and transportation costs for businesses.

Improving energy efficiency and reducing financial losses in the energy sector could help lower these costs and reduce pressure on households and businesses.

3. Agricultural Challenges and Food Inflation

Problems in the agricultural sector can affect people’s ability to afford food. Major crops such as wheat and cotton experienced decreased production in the previous financial year due to extreme weather events.

Food and Non-Alcoholic Beverages made up 34.6% of the national CPI basket between July and April 2024–25.[5] Water shortages, bad weather, increasing fertilizer costs, and poor storage facilities can cause problems with food supplies, leading to higher prices.

Improving crop yields, investing in water management and storage, and ensuring clear market regulations could help improve agricultural production and keep food supplies more stable.

4. Inflationary Expectations and Market Behaviour

If people expect prices to continue increasing, this can also contribute to inflation. The Inflation Expectations Index increased to 69.0 in July 2025, from 65.6 in June, according to the State Bank of Pakistan’s Consumer Confidence Survey.[6] This shows what people think about future inflation, not exactly how much they expect prices to increase.

Businesses may increase prices if they expect their production costs to rise. Workers may also ask for higher wages so they can continue affording their daily needs. While these decisions can contribute to further inflation, their impact depends on production costs, demand, and monetary policy.

Having credible monetary policies will help manage these expectations and maintain people’s confidence in the economy.

Human Impact

Inflation can cause problems for families who have to make difficult choices between essential expenses. If the prices of food, electricity, transportation, and healthcare increase while their income stays the same, people will have less money available for other needs and may struggle to maintain their standard of living.

For example, if essential items cost PKR 10,000 and prices increase by 20%, the same basket of goods will now cost PKR 12,000. If income has not changed, a household will either need to find an extra PKR 2,000 or reduce its spending in other areas.

Inflation can also cause savings to lose value when prices increase faster than the returns people earn on their money. Small businesses will also suffer from increased costs of inputs and production. People on lower incomes will struggle more to cope with unexpected expenses because they have less financial flexibility. This means inflation has a bigger impact on some groups in society than others.

How to Control Inflation in Pakistan

To control inflation, long-term changes will need to happen. Improving agriculture will increase productivity and help stabilize food supplies. Investing in water management and storage will also help. Improving local manufacturing can increase domestic supplies and reduce reliance on certain imports.

Having sustainable public finances, improving tax collection, and investing in productive areas will help reduce financial pressures. Improving the energy sector will also help reduce inefficiencies and unnecessary costs.

Having credible monetary policies will help keep prices stable. Supporting those most in need will help families manage rising living costs without putting too much pressure on public finances. Ensuring fair competition and monitoring markets can also help address specific pricing and supply issues.

There isn’t one solution to all the causes of inflation. Changes need to be made together and consistently to create a more stable economy.

Conclusion

Inflation in Pakistan is not just about increasing prices, but is also caused by deeper issues with public finances, energy, agriculture, and purchasing power. While the inflation rate is decreasing, it does not mean that essential goods and services have become affordable again.

Responsible economic management, increased local production, and supporting those most in need will help create long-term improvements. Overall, we should look at how people’s lives improve rather than just focusing on the statistics. We want to create an economy where people can afford essentials, savings can retain their value, and everyone can benefit from economic growth.
