The Economy of Creating a Problem

Delving deeper into the world of tactical consumer manipulation: how providers exploit loopholes to spread and capitalize on the marketed assumption of “necessity.”

RS
Rym Slimane Published November 1, 2026 · 5 min read

We often find ourselves drawn to products we never thought we needed—practical solutions to issues that only arise once they’re pointed out and deciphered. That “special” showerhead with an extra handle, sold at half the price, just to make sure you use both hands and not one when washing, because the latter feels too unhygienic nowadays. Or perhaps that mirror with the “unique” kind of glass that lets you pinpoint every last facial hair follicle, just so you’d remove each one and look brand new. Does that sound familiar? It may be due to the fact that it’s an ongoing pattern that’s been around for centuries—an invisible pillar in the economic sphere that clients are usually aware of but never question. “The Economy Of Creating A Problem” was and still is the operating mechanism in the offer scheme that manufactures demand where none existed in the first place.

The dependence effect in today’s market: The paradox

As stated in The Affluent Society (1958) by John Kenneth Galbraith, he maintained a strong standing against all economic dogmas disguised as “customer sovereignty” Especially when that same precept tends to appease one party of what was supposed to be a mutually beneficial trade. He argued that the system was never designed to cater to the customers’ authentic desires, but rather to serve the interests of the supplier through manufacturing those same wants during the production process, then labeling it as high independent demand and establishing the reversed chain from there, a scheme consisting of one threshold and too many hooks through the agency of advertisements and salesmanship, the bait is set and taken time and time again, due to the false sense of urgency.

One might dispute that the situation at its core is extremely nuanced, urgency is subjective, and can not adhere to one single standard, we also can not characterize desires into real and negligible ones. How can one differentiate? That was precisely Hayek’s objection–most explicitly in his essay The non sequitur of the “Dependence effect.” (1961) where he went as far as calling the distinction at hand inaccessible and irrational. Still, in this day and age, we can not bring ourselves to deny the apparent magnitude of dependency we currently live by. We’ve successfully progressed from TVs and printers to smartphones, digital platforms and the influence that constant structured algorithms carry. A far more complicated case that has gravely intensified ever since the broadcasting days Galbraith was originally analyzing, and as we can conclude in this contemporary era, the effect caused and is still causing a far-reaching ripple.

From product to norm: The real cost

If the dependence effect delineates how and why desires are generated, then that inevitably raises another dilemma: What are the real consequences to these disproportionate wants taking hold of the market? They are first presented as mere preferences, then as a solution among many others, then as the only one there is. As they defy all other brands and designs, they begin to harden their way into becoming the new norm. Some products do not even need to tick all of these boxes; it may depend on how socially maiming the stigma is, or the level of popularity it can reach. The showerhead will no longer register as an upgrade, the mirror will not come across as a luxury anymore. They will feel essential. Slowly the lines start to blur, and what seemed like a choice before purchase starts to feel like a forced rushed decision powered by strategic corporate gaslighting–which can eventually leave the client in a state of emotional dissatisfaction and persistent self blame.

Experimental psychologists Kathleen D. Vohs, Roy F. Baumeister, and Jason Chin addressed this phenomenon in their published paper (2007) titled “Feeling Duped: Emotional, Motivational, Cognitive Aspects of Being Exploited By Others” In this work, they described Feeling Duped as “an aversive emotional response arising from the perception of having been taken advantage of in an interpersonal transaction (primarily those involving economic exchange), partly as a result of one’s own decisions..” This reaction is distinct yet closely linked to the concept of “sugrophobia” or “The Fear of Being Suckered” a term the researchers coined for the motivation to avoid being tampered with again prior to finalizing more trades. Together, the two notions demonstrate the harm in the shift from product to norm, not only for the client but for both parties, by fostering vigilance and skepticism that may erode the consumer-provider relationship, which would most likely lead to rendering equitable dealings progressively more difficult to settle.

The asymmetrical pursuit of gain and its impact

The economy behind creating a problem is profoundly asymmetrical, the scales remain unbelievably unbalanced when the supplier keeps on making profit and flaunting their goods even after the purchaser’s double-edged payment, both with their money and emotional torment that is compiled with staying compliant and submissive to the new status quo. In a milder setting, what commenced as a fair deal under the guise of independence and free will, quickly morphed into “a scam, a rip-off, a catfish” or worse.

Additionally, it can be observed just how fast the tables flip when the economic becomes maliciously social, where products create standards, rarely if ever the reverse. This ultimately induces a status-seeking loop, an illusion sold by people in that same luxurious space to signal their wealth, as explained by the sociologist Thorstein Veblen in his “Theory of Conspicuous Consumption” from The Theory of the Leisure Class (1899) where he argued that in class-based societies, the pursuit of prestige is almost always positional–one person’s gain is another’s relative loss.

If you can sell the sting of lack, then the race will never end.

These redefined standards tend to reinforce hierarchical divisions whilst income continues to accrue to the providers, giving rise to an uneven advantage.

The takeaway

Thus, it may be inferred that the foundation of provoked needs is not based on deception alone, but rather constructed with assumptions of inadequacy first, which makes the “solution” appear before the problem does. This mechanism has been benefiting from cluelessness and implicit agreement for a substantial length of time, and the economy behind it can only collapse under the pressure of genuine autonomy. Consequently, the next time we encounter a product for which no reason for its creation instantly comes to mind, we should dismiss it outright. In doing so, we have effectively reclaimed our independence.
