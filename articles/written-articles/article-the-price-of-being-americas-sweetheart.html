The Price of Being America’s Sweetheart

Long hours, thin pay and a new union: what the Dallas Cowboys Cheerleaders reveal about opportunity cost and bargaining power.

SG
Saanchi Gabri Published November 17, 2026 · 5 min read

Dallas Cowboys Cheerleader Kelcey Wetterberg worked two jobs in one day. Her first shift started at 7:30 a.m. at a hospital. Her day ended around midnight at dance rehearsals.[1] Wetterberg is one of many cheerleaders featured on the Netflix documentary “America’s Sweethearts: Dallas Cowboys Cheerleaders.” The documentary shows the hours, pay, and expenses that the cheerleaders incur while representing one of the NFL’s most visible cheerleading teams.[2]

In the 2024 season, the cheerleaders practiced 20+ hours each week.[3] They performed at games and made many other appearances. Various cheerleaders on the squad had full-time jobs, but still managed to practice with the squad.[3] For Wetterberg, who is a pediatric nurse, balancing DCC with her full-time job is tough.[1]

“A lot of us work full-time jobs and come to DCC at night, so it can be really exhausting.”[1]

The Opportunity Cost of Practice

The time that cheerleaders spend practicing cannot be used for other paid work. Cheerleaders must plan their full-time jobs around the football schedule. This is referred to in economics as the opportunity cost.[4] For cheerleaders who work full-time jobs, this can mean losing hours they could have spent earning money by other means.

A Full-Time Commitment, Part-Time Pay

Former cheerleader Kat Puryear raised questions about whether the compensation cheerleaders receive is fair in comparison to the time they put in for the squad. “It’s a full-time commitment, but part-time pay,” said Puryear in a 2024 TikTok video. She went on to say, “It’s a lot of work.”[5] The cheerleaders were scheduled to work 21 days straight without a day off for practices, games and appearances.[3] Compensation for the Dallas Cowboys Cheerleaders has been a point of controversy even before the release of the Netflix series. Claiming she was paid less than minimum wage for the number of hours she worked as a cheerleader, Erica Wilkins sued the Dallas Cowboys in 2018.[6]

The cheerleaders were expected to pay for their own spray tans, hair extensions, and highlights for the signature look. In addition, the cheerleaders were expected to clean their own uniforms.[6] These expenses reduce the amount of money the cheerleaders actually earn from their work.

The lawsuit was settled in 2019. Pay for game days increased from $200 to $400 and hourly rate increased from $8 to $12.[3]

The Dallas Cowboys pay the Dallas Cowboys Cheerleaders for games, practices and appearances. The cheerleaders receive meals, memberships to a health club, and even services at a salon, not just their uniforms. However, the Dallas Cowboys did not disclose the full salary of the Dallas Cowboys Cheerleaders.[5] The cheerleaders also suffer from long-term injuries as dancers, and being a Dallas Cowboys Cheerleader comes with many physical demands.[3] Caroline Sundvold, a former DCC who now works in a corporate setting, postponed hip surgery until after her last season with the Cowboys. She had another foot surgery after she left the squad.[7] Puryear also dealt with injuries on both hips in her time as a cheerleader.[3]

Prestige Doesn’t Pay the Rent

The prestige of being on the team of the Dallas Cowboys Cheerleaders does not justify the financial expense of being on the team, Wilkins said in the article.[6]

“Yes, it is prestigious. But at the end of the day, prestige doesn’t pay my rent.”[6]

Who Holds the Bargaining Power?

Charlotte Jones, Chief Brand Officer for the Cowboys on America’s Sweethearts Season 1, understands the dancers’ concerns regarding their compensation, but they are on the squad for reasons other than money.[3] Many dream of being a Dallas Cowboys Cheerleader one day, but there are only 36 positions on the squad.[8] As an employer, the Cowboys have the opportunity to negotiate with several qualified candidates for the same job. Individual cheerleaders have less power to negotiate their pay, with so many people interested in joining.[9]

Megan McElaney, Armani Latimer, and Jada McLean, three of the veteran cheerleaders for the Dallas Cowboys Cheerleaders, fought for a pay raise in the second season of America’s Sweethearts in 2025.[10] According to McElaney, the dancers’ efforts were heard, and they were given a raise of approximately 400%.[10] However, the new agreement does not include health insurance for the dancers, a squad member told the New York Times.[11] Veteran dancers’ hourly pay was estimated to increase from $15 to $75.[11] “Happy” isn’t even the right word for it, McLean said. “I think I was just... kind of felt, like, a relief.”[11]

Bargaining as a Group

The Dallas Cowboys recognized the Dallas Cowboys Cheerleaders Association on October 23, 2026, as the organization’s collective bargaining representative for the squad of dancers.[12] For years the Dallas Cowboys Cheerleaders have been trying to get better compensation for their work.[12] The Dallas Cowboys and the Dallas Cowboys Cheerleaders Association are expected to negotiate the terms of employment for the dancers.[12] By negotiating as a group, the cheerleaders may have more bargaining power than they would if each cheerleader tried to negotiate by herself.[13] But no new terms have been announced yet.[12]
