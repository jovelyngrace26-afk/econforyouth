Will Money Still Matter in 2036? The Economics of an AI World

Elon Musk says money won’t matter in 2036. What the evidence on AI, jobs and inequality says about abundance, ownership and ambition.

SA
Saanvi Aditi Published November 21, 2026 · 5 min read

In a recent interview with The Economist, Elon Musk predicted that money will not matter in 2036. And everywhere on social media, people became crazy about this prediction. Some say this is not true, some say this is too crazy, and some say that it might be possible. But before we move on, we should look closely at the real prediction Elon Musk made.

What Did Elon Actually Say?

This is what Elon actually said:

“I’ll make another prediction. Money won’t matter in 2036. What do you want money for? You want money for goods and services, right? You want money for obviously food, housing, transport, entertainment. Well, if that is so abundant that there’s more, that the robots and AI are providing more goods and services than any human could possibly consume. What do you need money for? In that case?”[1]

So basically, he is saying that in future AI and robots will provide more goods and services than any human can possibly consume. So the resources will be so abundant that you will not need to buy and earn money. But if it’s true, what would humans do on Earth then? What will happen to jobs, wealth, and human ambition? While this sounds like a fiction movie, this prediction, I think, needs some serious research.

What if Work Becomes Optional?

The main argument of Elon’s prediction is that AI could make production so efficient that people would no longer need traditional jobs. People might spend less time earning a living and more time pursuing education, creativity, research, or personal interests. Goods and services could become cheaper and more widely available. However, this is not simple in the real future. A 2024 International Monetary Fund (IMF) analysis estimated that almost 40% of global employment is exposed to AI. Here exposure doesn’t mean that all these jobs will disappear, but AI here can help workers to perform more efficiently.[2]

The 2026 International Labour Organization (ILO) research study found that large-scale job displacement from AI remains limited so far. Although productivity improvements are showing, appearing, reported time savings have not yet consistently translated into higher output, wages, or employment.[3] It means that AI is changing work, but a world without human employment remains a prediction rather than an established outcome.

If Machines Create Goods, Who Gets It?

I know some people say that AI can perform many tasks more efficiently than humans, and this is true, that AI can perform many tasks that humans may take more time than AI. But even if AI creates huge amounts of wealth, goods and services, there is no guarantee that everyone will benefit equally. This question is more about economics than technology. To connect with this, think of the paid plans of ChatGPT. Not everybody has these paid plans. Most people use free plans or cheaper plans of ChatGPT. Result? few get more efficient AI results, while others get general results.

The same happens with companies and businesses. Let’s say a company owns thousands of AI-powered machines. These machines can produce a huge amount of goods with very little human labor. Of course, the company will earn profit, but its workforce may no longer be needed. If ownership remains concentrated among a small group, that group could receive most of the economic benefits, while others struggle to afford what is produced.

There was a very useful warning in the 2026 IMF research paper, Aggregate Gains from AI and their Distribution. Using AI users’ data across more than 100 countries, the researchers estimated that the reinforced equivalent of time saved through AI could reach approximately $2.7 trillion annually under their assumptions. But here these gains were distributed unevenly, favoring high-paying occupations and wealthier economies. And this alone represents the estimated value of time saved, not an actual increase in global GDP.[4]

The OECD’s 2026 paper, Skills in the AI Age, also says that larger companies are adopting AI faster than many smaller businesses because they have greater financial resources and better access to skilled workers.[5] This is how inequality still exists even if AI became AGI or SI. Therefore, even if production becomes abundant, ownership and access could remain major economic problems. After all, economics deals with issues such as inequality and scarcity. Money might become less important for purchasing basic necessities, but it could still matter when people compete for limited resources and opportunities.

What About Ambition?

Jobs and employment isn’t just about income and stable salaries. It also gives people independence, identity, social connections, and a sense of achievement. If machines perform the most economically necessary tasks, people may need to find new reasons to work towards their goals. Ambition is, however, a different thing. Ambition doesn’t exist because people need money. Think of scientists who pursue discoveries not to earn money, but to bring a change in humanity. Artists create their artwork not to majorly sell their paintings, but to show their creativity to the world. Entrepreneurs build businesses because they want to solve problems. In an AI world, human achievement might become less about earning a salary, but more about creating something meaningful for humanity.

Abundance Does Not Mean Equality

The fun fact is, in 1930, John Maynard Keynes wrote an essay about Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren. He explored how technological progress could reduce the amount of work needed to satisfy human needs.[6] Almost a century later, his prediction remains relevant, but economic insecurity and inequality have not disappeared.

So, Elon’s prediction may become possible in some areas, but technological progress alone cannot guarantee it. The future still depends on who owns productive technology, how its benefits are distributed, and whether governments ensure that ordinary people can access the opportunities.

Ultimately, AI may reduce the importance of jobs and make money less necessary for survival. However, making money irrelevant is such a bigger challenge than making machines capable of doing human work. The real measure of an advanced economy should not simply be how many goods machines can produce, but whether the abundance improves the lives of the people who use it.
